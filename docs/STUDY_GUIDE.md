## Energy, ATP & redox

Cells transfer energy through coupled reactions, and electron carriers connect fuel breakdown with ATP production.

**What:** Cells transfer energy through coupled reactions, and electron carriers connect fuel breakdown with ATP production.

**Where:** ATP coupling occurs throughout the cell; respiratory pathways span cytosol and mitochondria.

**Why:** Many cellular tasks are energetically unfavorable on their own. Coupling them to favorable reactions makes a combined process possible.

**Who:** ATP and ADP; redox enzymes; NAD+/NADH and FAD/FADH2.

**How:** Fuel oxidation transfers electrons to carriers. Energy is released in controlled steps and some is captured in ATP.

### Key ideas

- ATP hydrolysis can be coupled to transport, movement or synthesis; ATP is a transferable energy currency rather than long-term fuel storage.
- Oxidation means losing electrons; reduction means gaining electrons. These processes occur together in a redox reaction.
- NAD+ accepts electrons and becomes NADH. NADH later donates electrons and is oxidized back to NAD+.
- Enzymes lower activation barriers. They do not change the overall free-energy difference or create energy.

### Connect the steps

1. **Oxidize fuel** — Electrons are transferred from fuel molecules to electron carriers. Energy is captured gradually rather than released in one uncontrolled reaction.
2. **Capture usable energy** — ATP and reduced carriers store part of the transferred chemical energy. Carriers connect reactions occurring at different times and locations.
3. **Couple cellular work** — ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an otherwise unfavorable cellular process. Coupling links the energy-releasing and energy-requiring reactions.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Breaking an ATP bond alone releases energy.

**Mechanism:** Breaking a bond requires energy. ATP hydrolysis is favorable because the complete reaction produces more stable products.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ATP cycle | ATP → ADP + Pi | Work is coupled to the full reaction |
| Carrier cycle | NAD+ ↔ NADH | Electron transfer connects pathways |

## Glycolysis: one glucose, two pyruvates

Glycolysis splits one six-carbon glucose into two three-carbon pyruvates and captures ATP and NADH.

**What:** Glycolysis splits one six-carbon glucose into two three-carbon pyruvates and captures ATP and NADH.

**Where:** The cytosol, outside the mitochondrial matrix.

**Why:** It provides an initial way to extract energy from glucose, including when oxygen is unavailable.

**Who:** Glucose, ATP, ADP, NAD+, enzymes and two three-carbon intermediates.

**How:** An investment phase uses two ATP. A payoff phase produces four ATP and two NADH per glucose.

### Key ideas

- Net yield per glucose: two pyruvates, two ATP and two NADH.
- ATP is formed by substrate-level phosphorylation: a phosphate is transferred from an intermediate to ADP.
- Glycolysis does not directly require O2, but it requires a supply of NAD+.
- Carbon remains in the two pyruvates; glycolysis does not release CO2.

### Connect the steps

1. **Invest energy** — Two ATP are consumed as glucose is phosphorylated and prepared for splitting. Phosphorylation helps keep glucose in the cell and prepares it for later reactions.
2. **Split and oxidize** — The six-carbon pathway becomes two three-carbon pathways; NAD+ is reduced. Two parallel payoff sequences account for the doubled product numbers.
3. **Collect the payoff** — Four ATP are formed and two pyruvates remain. Net ATP equals four produced minus two invested. Net yield accounts for the earlier investment.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Glycolysis makes four net ATP.

**Mechanism:** It produces four ATP but consumes two, so the net yield is two ATP per glucose.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon | 1 glucose (6 C) → 2 pyruvates (3 C each) | No CO2 released |
| ATP | 2 consumed; 4 produced | Net 2 ATP |
| Carriers | 2 NAD+ → 2 NADH | Per glucose |

## Pyruvate oxidation: the bridge

Pyruvate oxidation links glycolysis to the citric acid cycle by producing acetyl-CoA.

**What:** Pyruvate oxidation links glycolysis to the citric acid cycle by producing acetyl-CoA.

**Where:** In the mitochondrial matrix of eukaryotic cells.

**Why:** The acetyl group can enter the cycle, while electrons are captured in NADH.

**Who:** Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, pyruvate, NAD+ and coenzyme A.

**How:** Each three-carbon pyruvate loses one carbon as CO2. Its remaining two-carbon acetyl group attaches to CoA.

### Key ideas

- Per glucose, two pyruvates produce two acetyl-CoA, two CO2 and two NADH.
- This bridge reaction does not directly make ATP.
- Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group; its attachment helps make subsequent transfer energetically useful.
- The two acetyl carbons enter the citric acid cycle; pyruvate itself does not enter the cycle as the starting input.

### Connect the steps

1. **Release one carbon** — One carbon from each pyruvate leaves as CO2. This connects three-carbon pyruvate with the two-carbon acetyl group.
2. **Capture electrons** — NAD+ is reduced to NADH. Oxidation energy is transferred to an electron carrier.
3. **Attach coenzyme A** — The acetyl group is linked to coenzyme A. Acetyl-CoA transfers its acetyl group into the next pathway.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Pyruvate oxidation directly makes ATP.

**Mechanism:** It makes NADH and acetyl-CoA but no ATP directly.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per pyruvate | 1 acetyl-CoA; 1 CO2; 1 NADH | No direct ATP |
| Per glucose | 2 acetyl-CoA; 2 CO2; 2 NADH | Two bridge reactions |

## The citric acid cycle

The cycle oxidizes acetyl groups and regenerates oxaloacetate while loading NADH and FADH2.

**What:** The cycle oxidizes acetyl groups and regenerates oxaloacetate while loading NADH and FADH2.

**Where:** Most reactions occur in the mitochondrial matrix; succinate dehydrogenase is associated with the inner membrane.

**Why:** Much of the captured energy is carried onward as reduced electron carriers, rather than direct ATP.

**Who:** Acetyl-CoA, oxaloacetate, cycle enzymes, NAD+, FAD and ADP/GDP.

**How:** Two acetyl carbons join four-carbon oxaloacetate. A series of reactions releases CO2 and restores the four-carbon acceptor.

### Key ideas

- One turn per acetyl-CoA yields three NADH, one FADH2, one ATP or GTP, and two CO2.
- Two turns per glucose yield six NADH, two FADH2, two ATP/GTP and four CO2.
- Oxaloacetate is regenerated; it is not consumed once for every turn.
- The cycle does not directly consume O2, but carrier recycling normally depends on oxygen-linked respiration.

### Connect the steps

1. **Join the cycle** — A two-carbon acetyl group combines with four-carbon oxaloacetate. The six-carbon product begins a cycle that can be repeated.
2. **Oxidize and capture** — Reactions release CO2 and reduce NAD+ and FAD; substrate-level phosphorylation forms ATP or GTP. Reduced carriers capture most of the pathway’s usable energy.
3. **Regenerate the acceptor** — Four-carbon oxaloacetate is restored. Regeneration allows another acetyl group to enter.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** One glucose produces only one turn of the cycle.

**Mechanism:** One glucose normally supplies two acetyl-CoA, so the cycle turns twice.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One turn | 3 NADH; 1 FADH2; 1 ATP/GTP; 2 CO2 | Per acetyl-CoA |
| Two turns | 6 NADH; 2 FADH2; 2 ATP/GTP; 4 CO2 | Per glucose |

## Electron transport: donor to oxygen

The respiratory electron transport chain transfers electrons to oxygen and uses released energy to build a proton gradient.

**What:** The respiratory electron transport chain transfers electrons to oxygen and uses released energy to build a proton gradient.

**Where:** The mitochondrial inner membrane; protons are moved from the matrix into the intermembrane space.

**Why:** Controlled electron transfer stores energy as an electrochemical gradient that ATP synthase can use.

**Who:** NADH, FAD-linked electrons, complexes I–IV, ubiquinone, cytochrome c and O2.

**How:** NADH donates to complex I. Electrons from succinate enter via complex II and Q. Q → III → cytochrome c → IV transfers electrons to O2.

### Key ideas

- Complexes I, III and IV contribute to proton pumping; complex II does not pump protons.
- Ubiquinone (Q) moves within the membrane; cytochrome c moves on the intermembrane-space side.
- Oxygen accepts electrons at the end of the chain and is reduced to water.
- Electron transport establishes the gradient; ATP synthase is a separate complex that uses the gradient.
- The uploaded clips are brief visual demonstrations. They are accompanied here by complete explanatory text.

### Connect the steps

1. **Donate electrons** — NADH is oxidized at complex I. FAD-linked electrons can enter through complex II toward Q. Entry points matter: bypassing complex I reduces the proton translocation associated with those electrons.
2. **Transfer and pump** — Q and cytochrome c connect large complexes. Transfer energy supports proton movement into the intermembrane space. This separates proton concentration and charge across an intact membrane.
3. **Reduce oxygen** — Complex IV transfers electrons to O2, forming water. A terminal acceptor permits continuing electron flow and carrier regeneration.
4. **Connect to ATP synthesis** — Protons can return to the matrix through ATP synthase. The gradient, rather than a direct electron-to-ATP transfer, powers ATP synthesis.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Complex II pumps protons just like complex I.

**Mechanism:** Complex II passes electrons toward Q but does not pump protons across the inner membrane.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electron donors | NADH; FAD-linked electrons | Different chain entry points |
| Terminal acceptor | O2 → H2O | At complex IV |
| Proton movement | Matrix → intermembrane space | By the chain; return through ATP synthase |

## Chemiosmosis & ATP synthase

Proton flow through ATP synthase couples an electrochemical gradient to ATP production.

**What:** Proton flow through ATP synthase couples an electrochemical gradient to ATP production.

**Where:** Across the mitochondrial inner membrane; the catalytic ATP synthase head faces the matrix.

**Why:** A membrane gradient lets many electron-transfer steps power a shared ATP-producing mechanism.

**Who:** ATP synthase, H+, ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi).

**How:** The respiratory chain establishes the gradient. H+ returns down its electrochemical gradient through ATP synthase; conformational changes drive ATP formation.

### Key ideas

- The proton-motive force includes both a concentration difference and an electrical potential difference.
- A sufficiently intact membrane is essential; a proton leak dissipates the gradient.
- Oxidative phosphorylation combines electron transport with chemiosmosis. It differs from substrate-level phosphorylation.
- Aerobic glucose oxidation in eukaryotic cells often yields about 30–32 ATP; yield is not a single fixed number across all conditions.

### Connect the steps

1. **Build the gradient** — Electron transport moves H+ against its electrochemical gradient. Input energy is required to maintain a difference across the membrane.
2. **Let protons return** — H+ moves through ATP synthase toward the matrix. Controlled return captures energy that would otherwise dissipate.
3. **Make ATP** — ADP and Pi form ATP through the enzyme’s coupled conformational changes. The catalytic head converts the stored gradient energy into transferable chemical energy.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Electrons flow through ATP synthase to make ATP.

**Mechanism:** Protons flow through ATP synthase. Electrons pass through the electron transport chain.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chain | Electron transfer → proton gradient | Energy transfer |
| ATP synthase | ADP + Pi → ATP | Coupled to proton return |
| Uncoupling | Proton leak reduces ATP coupling | O2 consumption and ATP output can diverge |

## Fermentation & NAD+ recycling

Fermentation regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue when respiratory NADH oxidation is unavailable.

**What:** Fermentation regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue when respiratory NADH oxidation is unavailable.

**Where:** The cytosol.

**Why:** A finite NAD+ supply must be restored for continued glycolytic oxidation.

**Who:** NADH, pyruvate or a pyruvate-derived product, and fermentation enzymes.

**How:** In lactate fermentation, pyruvate accepts electrons from NADH. In alcohol fermentation, pyruvate loses CO2 and acetaldehyde accepts electrons.

### Key ideas

- Fermentation’s essential role is NAD+ regeneration; it makes no additional ATP beyond glycolysis in these pathways.
- The net ATP yield associated with glycolysis plus lactate/alcohol fermentation is two ATP per glucose.
- Lactate fermentation does not release CO2 in its pyruvate-to-lactate step; alcohol fermentation does.
- Anaerobic respiration uses an electron transport chain with a terminal acceptor other than O2; it is different from fermentation.

### Connect the steps

1. **Produce pyruvate and NADH** — Glycolysis supplies both while making net two ATP. Glycolysis depends on recycled NAD+.
2. **Transfer electrons to an organic acceptor** — NADH reduces pyruvate or acetaldehyde. Reduction oxidizes NADH back to NAD+.
3. **Recycle NAD+** — NAD+ returns to glycolysis. Regeneration sustains glycolytic ATP production without the aerobic respiratory chain.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Fermentation adds a large ATP payoff after glycolysis.

**Mechanism:** The fermentation steps recycle NAD+; the two net ATP come from glycolysis.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lactate route | Pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD+ | No CO2 in this conversion |
| Alcohol route | Pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO2; then ethanol | NAD+ regenerated |
| Net ATP | 2 per glucose | Produced by glycolysis |

## Other fuels & pathway regulation

Respiratory pathways accept several fuel types and respond to the cell’s energy needs.

**What:** Respiratory pathways accept several fuel types and respond to the cell’s energy needs.

**Where:** Entry points span glycolysis, acetyl-CoA formation and the citric acid cycle.

**Why:** Cells must coordinate energy extraction with fuel availability and biosynthesis.

**Who:** Carbohydrates, fatty acids, amino acids and regulatory enzymes.

**How:** Glycerol can enter glycolysis; fatty acids undergo beta-oxidation to acetyl-CoA; deaminated amino-acid carbon skeletons enter at various points.

### Key ideas

- Proteins generally require removal of amino groups before their carbon skeletons enter energy pathways.
- Fats can supply substantial chemical energy because many of their carbons are highly reduced.
- High ATP availability can inhibit key catabolic control steps; AMP/ADP can signal greater ATP demand.
- Pathway intermediates also support biosynthesis, so respiration is connected to many other cellular processes.

### Connect the steps

1. **Prepare the fuel** — Break larger nutrients into molecules that can enter shared pathways. Different nutrients require different initial processing.
2. **Join a shared pathway** — Carbon skeletons enter glycolysis, acetyl-CoA production or the cycle. Shared downstream steps allow flexible fuel use.
3. **Adjust the rate** — Regulatory signals alter enzyme activity as energy demand changes. Feedback helps avoid unnecessary breakdown when ATP is abundant.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Every respiratory fuel must first become glucose.

**Mechanism:** Several fuels enter downstream of glucose, including fatty-acid-derived acetyl-CoA.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fatty acids | Beta-oxidation → acetyl-CoA | Not necessarily glucose first |
| Amino acids | Deamination then pathway entry | Entry depends on the carbon skeleton |

## Chloroplast compartments

Chloroplasts separate light reactions in thylakoid membranes from carbon fixation in the stroma.

**What:** Chloroplasts separate light reactions in thylakoid membranes from carbon fixation in the stroma.

**Where:** Photosynthetic cells, especially leaf mesophyll cells in plants.

**Why:** Compartments organize reactions and allow a thylakoid proton gradient to form.

**Who:** Envelope membranes, stroma, thylakoid membranes, lumen, grana and lamellae.

**How:** Light-reaction proteins occupy thylakoid membranes. ATP and NADPH produced on the stromal side support the Calvin cycle in the stroma.

### Key ideas

- The envelope surrounds the organelle; it is different from the internal thylakoid membrane.
- A granum is a stack of thylakoids. The lumen is the space inside a thylakoid.
- The stroma is outside the thylakoid lumen and inside the inner envelope.
- Plants also carry out cellular respiration. Photosynthesis does not eliminate their need for mitochondria.

### Connect the steps

1. **Locate the envelope** — Find the double boundary around the chloroplast. It defines the organelle’s compartment.
2. **Locate grana and lumen** — Inspect the stacks of thylakoid discs and their internal spaces. Membrane and lumen are distinct structures with different roles.
3. **Locate the stroma** — Identify the fluid region surrounding the thylakoids. This is where the Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** The Calvin cycle takes place inside the thylakoid lumen.

**Mechanism:** It takes place in the stroma; the lumen participates in the proton gradient.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thylakoid membrane | Light reactions | Contains photosystems and carriers |
| Lumen | H+ accumulation | Inside thylakoids |
| Stroma | Calvin cycle | Outside thylakoid lumen |

## Light, pigments & photosystems

Pigments absorb selected wavelengths, and photosystems organize light capture and electron transfer.

**What:** Pigments absorb selected wavelengths, and photosystems organize light capture and electron transfer.

**Where:** Pigments are associated with photosystem proteins in thylakoid membranes.

**Why:** Light energy must be captured and converted into useful chemical processes.

**Who:** Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, accessory pigments, antenna complexes and reaction-center chlorophyll.

**How:** Antenna pigments transfer excitation energy toward a reaction center, where an excited electron is transferred to an acceptor.

### Key ideas

- Absorption spectra measure which wavelengths a pigment absorbs; action spectra measure the biological response to wavelengths.
- Accessory pigments broaden light capture and can help protect against excess light.
- Energy transfer between antenna pigments is not the same as the reaction center’s electron transfer.
- Photosystem II operates before photosystem I in linear electron flow; numbering reflects discovery, not pathway order.

### Connect the steps

1. **Absorb a photon** — A pigment absorbs light of a suitable wavelength. Only absorbed energy can contribute to excitation.
2. **Transfer excitation** — Antenna complexes funnel excitation toward the reaction center. Many pigments can support a common reaction-center event.
3. **Transfer an electron** — Reaction-center chlorophyll transfers an excited electron to an acceptor. Electron transfer converts a brief excited state into a useful chemical event.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Photosystem I always comes first because it is numbered I.

**Mechanism:** Linear electron flow begins at photosystem II and then reaches photosystem I.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absorption spectrum | Pigment light absorption | Physical measurement |
| Action spectrum | Photosynthetic response to wavelength | Biological measurement |

## Light reactions: ATP, NADPH & oxygen

Linear electron flow uses light and water to support ATP and NADPH production while releasing oxygen.

**What:** Linear electron flow uses light and water to support ATP and NADPH production while releasing oxygen.

**Where:** The thylakoid membrane; H+ accumulates in the lumen and returns toward the stroma through ATP synthase.

**Why:** ATP provides energy and NADPH provides reducing power for carbon fixation.

**Who:** PSII, water, plastoquinone, cytochrome b6f, plastocyanin, PSI, ferredoxin, NADP+ reductase and ATP synthase.

**How:** Light excites electrons twice. Water replaces electrons lost from PSII. Electrons ultimately reduce NADP+; proton accumulation drives photophosphorylation.

### Key ideas

- Released O2 comes from water oxidation, not from CO2.
- PSII → carrier chain → PSI → ferredoxin → NADP+ reduction describes linear electron flow.
- ATP synthase releases ATP on the stromal side; NADPH is also available to stromal reactions.
- Cyclic electron flow around PSI can help generate extra ATP without producing NADPH or O2 through that cyclic route.

### Connect the steps

1. **Replace PSII electrons** — Water oxidation supplies electrons, releases O2 and adds protons to the lumen. Electron donation permits continuing PSII activity.
2. **Transfer electrons and build a gradient** — Carriers connect PSII with PSI while proton accumulation increases. The gradient supports ATP production across the thylakoid membrane.
3. **Re-excite and reduce NADP+** — Light at PSI raises electron energy again; electrons ultimately reduce NADP+. NADPH supplies reducing power needed for carbon fixation.
4. **Make ATP** — H+ returns toward the stroma through ATP synthase. Photophosphorylation captures gradient energy as ATP.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** The oxygen released by photosynthesis comes from CO2.

**Mechanism:** Released molecular oxygen comes from water oxidized at photosystem II.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inputs | Light; H2O; NADP+; ADP + Pi | Linear flow |
| Outputs | O2; NADPH; ATP | For exchange and stromal metabolism |
| H+ route | Lumen → stroma through ATP synthase | Return direction |

## The Calvin cycle: fixation to G3P

The Calvin cycle incorporates CO2 into organic molecules using ATP and NADPH.

**What:** The Calvin cycle incorporates CO2 into organic molecules using ATP and NADPH.

**Where:** The chloroplast stroma.

**Why:** Carbon fixation builds organic carbon that can support sugars and other biosynthesis.

**Who:** Rubisco, RuBP, 3-PGA, G3P, ATP and NADPH.

**How:** CO2 is fixed to RuBP, intermediates are reduced to G3P, and most G3P-derived carbon regenerates RuBP.

### Key ideas

- Three CO2 entering the cycle support one net G3P output and consume nine ATP and six NADPH.
- The other five of the six generated G3P molecules are used to regenerate three RuBP molecules.
- One G3P has three carbons; glucose has six. Glucose is not the cycle’s immediate net product.
- Light-independent means photons are not direct reactants; it does not mean the cycle normally operates only at night.

### Connect the steps

1. **Fix carbon** — Rubisco catalyzes addition of CO2 to RuBP, producing 3-PGA after the unstable intermediate splits. Inorganic carbon becomes part of organic molecules.
2. **Reduce intermediates** — ATP and NADPH support conversion of 3-PGA toward G3P. The carbon products receive energy and reducing power.
3. **Regenerate RuBP** — Most G3P carbon is rearranged, with ATP input, to restore RuBP. Regeneration allows continued CO2 fixation.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** The Calvin cycle directly produces one glucose after each turn.

**Mechanism:** Its net exported product is G3P; three CO2 give one net G3P, and further reactions can build sugars.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| For 3 CO2 | 9 ATP + 6 NADPH consumed | 1 net G3P |
| Carbon bookkeeping | 6 G3P formed; 1 exits; 5 support regeneration | Three RuBP restored |

## Photorespiration: competing substrates

Rubisco can act with O2 instead of CO2, initiating photorespiration and reducing productive carbon fixation.

**What:** Rubisco can act with O2 instead of CO2, initiating photorespiration and reducing productive carbon fixation.

**Where:** The pathway spans chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria.

**Why:** Rubisco’s competing activities help explain why hot, dry conditions can lower photosynthetic efficiency.

**Who:** Rubisco, RuBP, CO2, O2 and enzymes that recover part of the resulting carbon.

**How:** Stomatal closure conserves water but can lower internal CO2. Oxygenation of RuBP becomes more competitive, and recovery costs energy while releasing some CO2.

### Key ideas

- Photorespiration differs from mitochondrial respiration and does not provide a comparable ATP payoff.
- Rubisco’s oxygenase activity competes with its carboxylase activity.
- Hot, dry conditions can promote stomatal closure and alter the internal CO2/O2 balance.
- C4 and CAM mechanisms help concentrate or conserve CO2 supply for productive fixation.

### Connect the steps

1. **Conserve water** — Stomata close under water stress. Closure reduces water loss but limits gas exchange.
2. **Change substrate competition** — Lower CO2 relative to O2 increases the chance of RuBP oxygenation. Rubisco can interact with either substrate.
3. **Recover carbon at a cost** — A multi-organelle pathway recovers some carbon while consuming energy and releasing CO2. Recovery is less productive than direct CO2 fixation.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Photorespiration is simply another name for ATP-producing respiration.

**Mechanism:** It is a distinct carbon-recovery pathway associated with Rubisco oxygenation and an energy cost.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carboxylase route | Rubisco + CO2 | Supports Calvin-cycle fixation |
| Oxygenase route | Rubisco + O2 | Initiates photorespiration |

## C4 & CAM: space versus time

C4 and CAM pathways help improve CO2 availability for the Calvin cycle in challenging environments.

**What:** C4 and CAM pathways help improve CO2 availability for the Calvin cycle in challenging environments.

**Where:** C4 separates initial fixation and the Calvin cycle across cell types; CAM separates them across times of day.

**Why:** These mechanisms can reduce photorespiration or water loss, but they have energetic and ecological tradeoffs.

**Who:** PEP carboxylase, four-carbon acids, Rubisco and specialized cells or storage patterns.

**How:** C4 plants deliver concentrated CO2 to bundle-sheath cells. CAM plants capture CO2 at night into organic acids, then release it for daytime Calvin-cycle activity.

### Key ideas

- C4: initial fixation typically occurs in mesophyll cells; CO2 is supplied to the Calvin cycle in bundle-sheath cells.
- CAM: stomata typically open at night; stored organic acids provide CO2 during daytime closure.
- Both still use the Calvin cycle; they do not replace it with a completely separate sugar-making pathway.
- The advantage depends on conditions; neither mechanism is universally best.

### Connect the steps

1. **Capture CO2 initially** — PEP carboxylase helps incorporate CO2 into four-carbon products. This supports efficient initial CO2 capture.
2. **Separate in space or time** — C4 uses different cell types; CAM uses different times of day. Different forms of separation fit different constraints.
3. **Supply the Calvin cycle** — CO2 is released near Rubisco for productive fixation. The Calvin cycle remains the downstream carbon-building pathway.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** C4 and CAM are two names for the same separation mechanism.

**Mechanism:** C4 primarily separates processes in space; CAM primarily separates them in time.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C4 | Mesophyll ↔ bundle sheath | Spatial separation |
| CAM | Night ↔ day | Temporal separation |

## Compare the two energy systems

Respiration and photosynthesis share electron transport and chemiosmosis, but their donors, acceptors and carbon roles differ.

**What:** Respiration and photosynthesis share electron transport and chemiosmosis, but their donors, acceptors and carbon roles differ.

**Where:** Respiratory inner membranes and photosynthetic thylakoid membranes.

**Why:** Comparison helps distinguish a shared mechanism from pathway-specific chemistry.

**Who:** Respiration: NADH/FAD-linked electrons and O2. Photosynthesis: water, light and NADP+ in linear flow.

**How:** Both use electron transfer to support a proton gradient. ATP synthase uses proton return, but carbon oxidation and carbon fixation have different roles.

### Key ideas

- Respiration generally oxidizes organic carbon; photosynthesis fixes inorganic carbon using energy captured from light.
- In respiration, O2 is consumed as the terminal electron acceptor. In oxygenic photosynthesis, O2 is released from water.
- Mitochondrial H+ accumulates in the intermembrane space; chloroplast H+ accumulates in the thylakoid lumen.
- Plants perform both pathways. Net gas exchange combines photosynthesis and respiration rather than proving only one occurs.

### Connect the steps

1. **Identify donor and acceptor** — Name the electron source and final acceptor for each pathway. Similar-looking membranes do not imply identical chemistry.
2. **Locate the gradient** — Find the high-H+ compartment and the direction of return. Compartment names prevent directional confusion.
3. **Connect energy and carbon** — Compare fuel oxidation with carbon fixation. The systems connect but are not simple reverse versions of every molecular step.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** Photosynthesis is cellular respiration run backward step by step.

**Mechanism:** Their overall carbon and gas relationships connect, but the pathways use different enzymes and mechanisms.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Respiration | NADH/FAD-linked donors → O2 | ATP; high H+ in intermembrane space |
| Linear photosynthesis | H2O donor → NADP+ | ATP and NADPH; high H+ in lumen |

## Read evidence, not just a diagram

Experiments test explanations by comparing controlled conditions and identifying what measurements can support.

**What:** Experiments test explanations by comparing controlled conditions and identifying what measurements can support.

**Where:** A leaf-disc assay, isolated organelles or a respirometer can investigate aspects of energy metabolism.

**Why:** A visual model proposes a mechanism; measured evidence is needed to evaluate it.

**Who:** Independent variable, dependent measurement, controls, replicates and alternative explanations.

**How:** Vary one defined condition, keep relevant conditions comparable, record repeated measurements, and interpret the measurement without claiming more than it demonstrates.

### Key ideas

- In a leaf-disc assay, flotation can reflect accumulated gas, but infiltration, density and other conditions affect results.
- Dissolved-O2 changes reflect net production or consumption under the experimental conditions.
- Controls help distinguish a treatment effect from background changes; biological replicates assess variation.
- A model is a simplification. An unexpected result may reveal a missing variable rather than validate or invalidate every part of a pathway.

### Connect the steps

1. **Define a testable question** — Specify the treatment and measurement before collecting data. A precise prediction makes evidence interpretable.
2. **Control and replicate** — Include an appropriate comparison and repeat independent samples. Controls and replication address different sources of uncertainty.
3. **Interpret limits** — Relate results to the measured outcome and consider competing causes. A correlation or model demonstration alone does not establish all causal details.

### Check a common misconception

**Mix-up:** One successful animation proves that the biological mechanism is correct.

**Mechanism:** An animation illustrates a proposal. Experiments and independent evidence are needed to test mechanisms.

### Quick bookkeeping

| Item | Change / output | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Measurement | Recorded response | Dependent variable |
| Interpretation | Evidence plus limitations | Avoid claiming an unmeasured result |

## Vocabulary

- **ATP:** Adenosine triphosphate: a molecule whose hydrolysis can be coupled to cellular work.
- **ADP:** Adenosine diphosphate; adding Pi with an energy input can regenerate ATP.
- **Pi:** Inorganic phosphate, a reactant in ATP formation from ADP.
- **NADH:** The reduced form of NAD+, carrying electrons toward respiratory pathways.
- **NADPH:** A reduced electron carrier supplying reducing power to biosynthesis, including Calvin-cycle reactions.
- **FADH2:** Reduced FAD; FAD-linked electrons can enter the respiratory chain at a different point from NADH.
- **Glycolysis:** Cytosolic glucose breakdown to two pyruvates, with net ATP and NADH capture.
- **Pyruvate:** A three-carbon product of glycolysis, converted to acetyl-CoA in aerobic glucose oxidation.
- **Acetyl-CoA:** A carrier of a two-carbon acetyl group entering the citric acid cycle.
- **Oxaloacetate:** The four-carbon acceptor regenerated in the citric acid cycle.
- **Matrix:** The compartment enclosed by the mitochondrial inner membrane.
- **Cristae:** Folds of the mitochondrial inner membrane that increase membrane area.
- **Intermembrane space:** The space between mitochondrial outer and inner membranes; the respiratory high-proton side.
- **Ubiquinone:** Q: a mobile electron carrier within the mitochondrial inner membrane.
- **Cytochrome c:** A mobile respiratory electron carrier on the intermembrane-space side of the inner membrane.
- **Chemiosmosis:** Energy coupling through ion movement down an electrochemical gradient across a membrane.
- **ATP synthase:** A membrane-associated enzyme coupling proton flow to ATP synthesis.
- **Fermentation:** A pathway regenerating NAD+ by reducing organic molecules, sustaining glycolysis.
- **Stroma:** The chloroplast fluid region outside thylakoid lumen spaces; location of the Calvin cycle.
- **Thylakoid:** An internal chloroplast membrane compartment containing light-reaction machinery.
- **Lumen:** The space inside a thylakoid, where protons accumulate during light reactions.
- **Granum:** A stack of thylakoid discs.
- **Rubisco:** The enzyme catalyzing CO2 fixation to RuBP; it can also catalyze RuBP oxygenation.
- **RuBP:** Ribulose bisphosphate, the five-carbon CO2 acceptor regenerated in the Calvin cycle.
- **G3P:** Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, the three-carbon net product exported from the Calvin cycle.
- **Photorespiration:** Carbon-recovery reactions following Rubisco oxygenation, with an energy cost and some carbon loss.
- **Substrate-level phosphorylation:** Direct transfer of a phosphate from a phosphorylated substrate to ADP or GDP.
- **Oxidative phosphorylation:** ATP production through respiratory electron transport and chemiosmosis.
- **Photophosphorylation:** ATP production using a proton gradient associated with photosynthetic electron flow.
- **Action spectrum:** A biological response plotted against light wavelength.
